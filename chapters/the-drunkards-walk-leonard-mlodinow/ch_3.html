Chapter 3: Finding Your Way Through a Space of Possibilities

Galileo and the Grand Duke of Tuscany

The Grand Duke of Tuscany approached Galileo with a puzzle that perplexed Italian gamblers. When rolling three six-sided dice, both 9 and 10 can be formed from exactly six different combinations of three numbers:

- **Sum of 9:** $(6,2,1), (5,3,1), (5,2,2), (4,4,1), (4,3,2), (3,3,3)$

- **Sum of 10:** $(6,3,1), (6,2,2), (5,4,1), (5,3,2), (4,4,2), (4,3,3)$

Since both sums can be partitioned into six integer triples, the Grand Duke reasoned that 9 and 10 should appear with identical frequency. Yet veteran gamblers noticed that 10 appeared slightly more often than 9.

Galileo recognized that one must count the total permutations in the sample space ($6 imes 6 imes 6 = 216$ equally likely outcomes):

- A combination with three distinct numbers, like $(6,3,1)$, can occur in $3! = 6$ ways.

- A combination with two identical numbers, like $(5,2,2)$, can occur in $3! / 2! = 3$ ways.

- A combination with three identical numbers, like $(3,3,3)$, can occur in only $1$ way.

Summing the permutations, Galileo proved that 10 has 27 favorable outcomes ($27/216 = 12.5\%$), whereas 9 has only 25 favorable outcomes ($25/216 = 11.57\%$). The gamblers' subtle intuition was validated by rigorous combinatorial counting.

The Monty Hall Paradox

On the game show *Let's Make a Deal*, host Monty Hall presents three closed doors. Behind one door is a sports car; behind the other two are goats.

1. You pick Door 1.

2. Monty, who knows what is behind every door, opens Door 3 to reveal a goat.

3. Monty asks: *"Do you want to stick with Door 1 or switch to Door 2?"*

Intuition tells most people that with two doors left, the odds are 50-50, so switching doesn't matter. In 1990, Marilyn vos Savant correctly stated in *Parade* magazine that you should always switch, because switching doubles your chances of winning ($2/3$ vs $1/3$). She received thousands of angry letters, including from PhD mathematicians asserting she was wrong.

Mlodinow demonstrates why Marilyn was right:

- When you first chose Door 1, the probability that the car was behind Door 1 was $1/3$.

- The probability that the car was behind Door 2 or Door 3 was $2/3$.

- Monty's action is not random: he *must* open a door with a goat. He filters the entire $2/3$ probability of the two unchosen doors into Door 2! Switching guarantees a $2/3$ win rate.
