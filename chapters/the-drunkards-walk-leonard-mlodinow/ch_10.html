Chapter 10: The Drunkard's Walk: Stepping Into the Future

The Random Walk Physics

In 1827, Scottish botanist Robert Brown watched pollen grains suspended in water through a microscope and noticed they were in continuous, jittery, erratic motion. In 1905, Albert Einstein proved that this Brownian motion was caused by millions of invisible water molecules colliding randomly with the pollen grain from all directions.

In mathematics, this is the classic **Drunkard's Walk**:

- A drunkard stands under a streetlamp. Every second, he takes one step either to the left or to the right, chosen at random.

- After $N$ steps, what is his expected net distance from the lamp?

- It is not zero, nor is it $N$. The average net distance is proportional to the **square root of $N$**:

$$D pprox \sqrt{N}$$

The Ultimate Takeaway: Persistence Multiplies Chance

Because outcomes in life are heavily influenced by the random drift of the drunkard's walk, Mlodinow arrives at an uplifting and practical conclusion:

1. **Do Not Confuse Outcome with Skill:** An unlucky outcome does not mean you made a bad decision; a lucky outcome does not make you a visionary. Judge yourself by the integrity of your process, not by temporary noise.

2. **Increase Your Swings at the Bat:** Since breakthrough success depends on the intersection of prepared skill and random opportunity, the single variable you control is **the number of trials ($N$)**.

- Thomas Edison tested thousands of materials before finding the carbon filament.

- J.K. Rowling was rejected by 12 major publishers before an editor at Bloomsbury handed the manuscript to his 8-year-old daughter.

3. **Walk with Grace:** Understanding randomness liberates you from self-blame during setbacks and from arrogance during windfalls. You keep walking, taking another step into the open future.
