Chapter 9: Illusions of Patterns and Patterns of Illusion

Regression to the Mean

Francis Galton studied the heights of children compared to their parents. He noticed that exceptionally tall parents tended to have children who were shorter than they were, while exceptionally short parents tended to have children who were taller than they were.

Galton called this phenomenon **Regression toward Mediocrity** (now known as **Regression to the Mean**):

*Extreme performance—whether exceptionally good or exceptionally bad—is almost always the product of a baseline of skill combined with exceptional luck. Because extreme luck rarely repeats twice in a row, the next performance naturally regresses toward the average.*

The Flight Instructor Fallacy

Daniel Kahneman discovered this bias while teaching Israeli Air Force flight instructors:

- Instructors noticed that when they praised a pilot for a brilliant maneuver, his next flight was usually worse.

- When they screamed at a pilot for a botched maneuver, his next flight was usually better.

- The instructors concluded that praise breeds complacency and screaming breeds excellence!

In truth, a brilliant flight was already an extreme positive outlier that was bound to regress downward anyway, while a terrible flight was an extreme negative outlier that was bound to regress upward. The screaming had zero effect; regression to the mean created the optical illusion that punishment worked.
