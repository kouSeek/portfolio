Chapter 7: Measurement and the Law of Errors

The Bell Curve (Gauss and the Lost Asteroid Ceres)

In 1801, Italian astronomer Giuseppe Piazzi spotted a tiny heavenly body—the dwarf planet Ceres—before it vanished into the glare of the sun. Astronomers across Europe despaired of ever finding it again based on just 40 days of scattered, conflicting observational data.

A 24-year-old Carl Friedrich Gauss stepped forward. Instead of averaging the conflicting measurements, Gauss developed the **Method of Least Squares** and formalized the **Normal Distribution** (the Gaussian Bell Curve):

$$f(x) =

rac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-

rac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$

Gauss realized that every measurement contains random physical disturbances (atmospheric turbulence, mechanical gear backlash, human reaction time). If these individual disturbances are independent, positive and negative errors cancel each other out, while small errors occur frequently and huge errors occur rarely. Using this curve, Gauss calculated the exact patch of sky where Ceres would reappear—and German astronomers pointed their telescopes directly to the coordinates and rediscovered Ceres on New Year's Eve, 1801!
