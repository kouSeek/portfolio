Jacob Bernoulli's Ars Conjectandi

Jacob Bernoulli spent over twenty years proving mathematically what common sense intuited: that as the number of random trials increases, the observed frequency of an event converges to its theoretical probability:

$$\lim_{n o \infty} P\left(\left|

rac{X_n}{n} - p

ight| < \epsilon

ight) = 1$$

Bernoulli was ecstatic, believing he had discovered a universal mathematical proof of divine providence. But Bernoulli's theorem requires **very large numbers**—often tens of thousands of observations—before the sample proportion reliably stabilizes within a tight margin of error.

The Law of Small Numbers (Tversky & Kahneman)

The psychological catastrophe occurs because humans expect short sequences of random events to look like the long-term distribution.

If a coin is flipped 6 times, people believe `H-T-H-T-T-H` is much more likely than `H-H-H-H-H-H`, even though both specific sequences have the exact same probability:

$$\left(

rac{1}{2}

ight)^6 =

rac{1}{64}$$

In business and hiring, this fallacy wreaks havoc:

- An investor evaluates a venture fund manager over 3 years (a sample of 12 investments). If 9 succeed, the manager is hailed as a genius.

- A pharmaceutical company tests a drug on 20 patients. If 16 improve, the drug is rushed to market.

- In both cases, the sample size is far too small to rule out pure random variation. What looks like sustained mastery or therapeutic efficacy is often just the expected noise of a small sample.
